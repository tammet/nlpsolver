A rule was judged to need a restriction before it can be used. Your job is to
pick that restriction from a fixed list, or to say it is not there.

HOW TO READ THE LOGIC

These are the notation's conventions.  They are facts about the notation, not
hints about this problem.

  isa(TYPE, THING)
      The FIRST argument names the type; the second is the individual or
      variable that is of that type.  `isa(bird, ?X)` says ?X is a bird.  It
      does NOT say bird is an ?X.

  is rel2(RELATION, X, Y)
      The first argument names the relation; X and Y are its two participants,
      in that order.  `is rel2(member of, ?X, C)` says ?X is a member of C.

  has property(PROPERTY, X)
      The first argument names the property; X is what has it.

  has degree property(PROPERTY, X, DEGREE, CLASS)
      As above, with a degree and the comparison class it belongs to.

  has type(E, WORD) / has actor(E, X) / has target(E, X)
      An event E, what kind it is, and who fills each role.

  forall ?X . forall ?Y . ( BODY -> normally HEAD )
      The rule fires for EVERY combination of ?X and ?Y that makes BODY true,
      and concludes HEAD for that combination.  It does NOT say that one thing
      stands in the relation to all of them.  `forall ?X . forall ?Y . (
      loves(?X, ?Y) & animal(?Y) -> normally animal_lover(?X) )` fires once for
      each pair where ?X loves an animal ?Y; it does not require ?X to love
      every animal.

  normally
      The conclusion holds by default and can be overridden by a contrary fact.
      It is not a claim of necessity.

  A name like "Tom 1" or a URL is one specific individual.  A `?X` is a
  variable: the rule speaks about everything that matches it.

WHAT A CHOSEN CONDITION MEANS

Exactly one thing. If the rule is

    LEFT  ->  HEAD

and you choose G2, the rule becomes

    LEFT AND G2  ->  HEAD

The rule then applies in fewer situations than before, and concludes the same
thing in those situations.

It does NOT mean any of these:

    "unless G2"
    "G2 overrides the rule"
    "G2 blocks HEAD"
    "G2 is an exception to the rule"

There is no way to express an exception here. If what you want is "the rule
holds except when G2", that restriction is not available and you should say so.
In particular, never choose a condition that says the opposite of what the rule
concludes: adding it would produce a rule saying that whenever its own
conclusion is false, its conclusion holds.

WHAT TO PICK

Pick the condition that makes the rule say what the English actually licenses.
Prefer the smallest set that does that. At most three.

A condition is worth choosing only if it restricts something. A condition
mentioning none of the rule's terms restricts nothing and will be refused.

If the restriction the rule needs is not offered, say UNAVAILABLE. That is a
normal answer, not a failure — a rule that cannot be restricted properly should
not be used at all.

HOW TO ANSWER

Reason in plain prose first. Then end with exactly these two lines and nothing
after them:

GUARDS: G1, G3   |   UNAVAILABLE   |   NONE
WHY: one sentence

Use NONE only if you conclude the rule needed no restriction after all. Only
these lines are read.
