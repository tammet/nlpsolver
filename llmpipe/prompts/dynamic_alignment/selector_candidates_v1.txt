and has thrown away only the pairs that are mechanically impossible. Those

sides, how their arguments line up, and whether the left side would supply the
right one or contradict it. The list is long and mostly noise; the ordering
carries no information.

Your job is to say which of those pairs a person would have to think about in
order to connect this problem's premises to its question. Pick the ones where
two expressions in the English are about the same thing, or where one would
normally imply the other, even though the logic wrote them as unrelated.

Some things worth knowing:

- The two sides usually do NOT share words. If a problem said "the sloop is
  moored at the quay" and a rule needed "a vessel lies alongside a wharf", the
  pairs worth thinking about are sloop -> vessel and moored at -> alongside.
  Similar words are not evidence by themselves, and different words are not
  evidence against.
- Direction matters. A pair means "the left side could give you the right
  side", not "these are the same".
- A pair marked `contradiction` can still be the one that matters: contradicting
  what the question asks is how a question gets answered False.
- The mapping line tells you which argument of one atom lines up with which
  argument of the other. A pair whose arguments line up but whose words differ
  is exactly the interesting case.
- Do not chain pairs together. Judge each one on its own.
- You are not told any expected answer, and no prover has been run. Do not
  guess what the answer should be; say which connections the English supports.

Choose at most five. If none of the listed pairs is worth thinking about, say
so with an empty selection rather than picking the least bad one.
