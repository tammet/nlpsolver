You are looking at one reasoning problem that a logic pipeline failed to answer,
and at the expressions its prover needed along the way.

The pipeline translated the English into logic and asked a prover the question.
The prover returned nothing. That usually means one of two things: the English
says something the translation did not capture, or the translation used two
different logical shapes for what is really the same idea, so the pieces never
met.

Below you are given:

  * the original English;
  * the atoms the translation produced, sentence by sentence, with the role of
    each sentence — a fact, a rule, or the question;
  * the expressions the prover needed while working backwards from the
    question, collapsed into semantic groups.

A GROUP is one content expression: a predicate, the word it is about, and its
participants. Many mechanical variants of the same expression — different
context arguments, different internal event terms, different placeholder
witnesses — are folded into one group, and the count of those variants is
shown. `?` in a group means a participant the prover had not pinned down.

BOTH DIRECTIONS ARE SHOWN

The groups are listed under two headings. One heading holds what the prover
would need in order to SUPPLY the question's claim — the expressions that would
let it answer yes. The other holds what it would need in order to CONTRADICT the
claim — the expressions that would let it answer no. Both are shown on purpose,
and both are open: an unanswered question is not a hint that the answer is yes.
Choose from either heading, or from both.

QUESTIONS ARE NOT ASSERTIONS

Some groups come from the question rather than from a statement. A question
tells you which expression and sense is at issue. It is not evidence that the
expression is true, and it is not evidence that it is false. Never treat the
question's wording as a fact.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Pick the groups where a REPRESENTATION MISMATCH looks likely: places where the
English plainly supports the content but the logic is using a different shape
for it. The recurring kinds are:

  * the same idea written two ways — an event in one place and a noun in
    another, a relation in one place and a property in another;
  * a word that stands to another as a kind to its category, or as a compound
    to its head, where the logic keeps them as unrelated predicates;
  * the same relation with its participants in different positions, or with an
    extra or missing participant;
  * a group whose content the English clearly states, expressed with a
    predicate or word the rest of the logic never uses.

Do not pick a group merely because it looks important, or because it comes from
the question. Pick the ones where you can point at the English and say: this is
the same thing said differently.

WHAT YOUR CHOICE MEANS

Your choice is PRIORITIZATION ONLY. It decides which expressions a later stage
looks at first; it is not approval of anything, it does not assert that a rule
about the group is true, and nothing you pick here is added to the problem. A
separate stage judges whether any rule that comes out of this is acceptable, and
it can reject all of them. So prefer to include a group you are unsure about
over leaving the list short: the cost of a wrong inclusion is one wasted look,
and the cost of a wrong exclusion is that nothing downstream can ever consider
it.

HOW TO ANSWER

Explain your reasoning in ordinary prose, as much or as little as you need.
Then end with exactly one line, and nothing after it:

SELECTED: G2,G7,G11

At most eight group ids. Use the ids exactly as they are printed. Only that
final line is read.
