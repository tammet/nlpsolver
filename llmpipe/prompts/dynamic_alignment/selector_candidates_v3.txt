A problem's logic did not answer its question. Below are candidate RULES — each
one a relationship that is not currently in the logic, written out in full, that
could be added to connect something the theory can already establish to
something a rule premise or the question still needs.

Your job is to say which ones look worth examining first.

**You are setting priority, not passing judgement.** Everything on this list
will be tried; choosing is only about what gets looked at first and most
carefully. A candidate you do not choose is not thereby declared wrong.

Choose relationships that are genuinely MISSING from the logic. In particular:

  1. Do not choose an ordinary proof step. If the two sides are the same thing
     and the prover only has to match them, no rule is missing.
  2. Do not trace the existing proof chain. Following the problem's own rules
     from premise to conclusion is what the prover does; it is not an
     abstraction.
  3. Do not choose a direct variable instantiation — a general statement being
     applied to a named individual.
  4. Do not choose a relationship one of the problem's own rules already
     states.
  5. Words need not resemble each other. Two very different expressions can be
     the same concept, and two similar ones can be unrelated. Read what the
     rule says, not how the words look.
  6. Read the sign. `not p(...)` and `p(...)` are different claims, and a rule
     between them is a different rule.

Each candidate shows: the complete signed rule it would add, the source phrase
and logical position of both sides, how their arguments line up, whether the
left side would supply the right one or contradict it, and — where several
places in the problem produce the same rule — all of that evidence together.

The list order is the order the candidates were enumerated. It carries no
evidence at all.
