A pipeline translated a short passage into logic, sentence by sentence. Each
sentence was translated on its own, so a variable in one sentence has nothing to
do with a variable in another: they are separate quantifiers that happen to be
written with letters.

To build a rule out of atoms from two different sentences, code sometimes decides
that a term in one sentence and a term in another refer to the same thing. That
decision is not in the logic. It is an assumption code made so the pieces would
join, and it is the only thing standing between the rule and the two unrelated
sentences it was assembled from.

You are asked about those decisions and nothing else. Not whether the rule is
useful, not whether the rule is true, not whether a prover could do anything
with it. Only: reading the passage, do those two terms refer to the same thing?

For each identification you are shown the sentence each term came from, the atom
it appears in, the guard the identification attaches, and the complete rule that
results.

ANSWER ONE OF THESE FOR EACH

  SUPPORTED_IDENTIFICATION
      The passage makes the two terms the same thing. A reader following the
      text would take them as one.
  POSSIBLE_IDENTIFICATION
      They could be the same and the passage does not settle it.
  UNSUPPORTED_IDENTIFICATION
      The passage gives no reason to join them, or they are plainly different
      things.

Being about the same KIND of thing is not enough. Two sentences can both talk
about organisms without talking about the same organism.

HOW TO ANSWER

Write a sentence of reasoning if you want. Then end with exactly one line per
identification, and nothing after them:

IDENT: A1 = POSSIBLE_IDENTIFICATION; reason=both sentences discuss cells, but neither says they are the same cell

Use the ids exactly as printed. Only those lines are read; nothing is taken from
prose, and an identification you do not mention is recorded as unsettled rather
than as approved.
