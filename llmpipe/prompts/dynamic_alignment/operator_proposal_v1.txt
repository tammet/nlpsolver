A logic pipeline translated a reasoning problem into logic and asked a prover
the question. The prover returned nothing. Below you are shown the problem, the
atoms the translation produced, the words the problem uses, and the expressions
the prover needed and could not get.

Usually one of two things has gone wrong. The English says something the
translation never wrote down. Or the translation wrote the same idea in two
different shapes, so the two halves never met.

Your job is to propose the rule that would join them — but you do not write the
rule. You name its parts, and code builds it.

WHAT YOU NAME

  a target      what the rule should CONCLUDE. Either a group id (G3), a word
                id (L7), or the label of an occurrence (O7.label).
  sources       the occurrence ids the rule is built from (O2, O7). At most
                three.
  an operator   one name from the list at the end of the problem.
  conditions    occurrence ids that must also hold for the rule to fire, or
                NONE. At most two.

You never write a quantified formula, and you never choose which argument lines
up with which. Code derives every compatible argument mapping from the
occurrences you name, connects the variables, quantifies, checks the result and
refuses it if it does not hold together. If more than one mapping survives, all
of them are built and reported. Naming is your job; serialising is not.

QUESTIONS ARE NOT ASSERTIONS

Lines marked `question` come from the question. They tell you which expression
and which sense is at issue. They are not evidence that the expression is true
and not evidence that it is false. A word may appear only in the question — that
makes it available to name, not something the problem asserts.

WORDS THAT ARE ONLY ARGUMENTS

A word marked `argument only` is one the logic can mention but nothing can BE:
`organic matter` may appear only as the second argument of `source of`. If the
English supports it, promoting such a word into a class of its own is a real
construction, and there is an operator for it. The same is true of a word that
exists in the problem as a verb but needs to be a noun, and of a rule whose
label position holds a variable rather than a word.

WHAT MAKES A PROPOSAL GOOD

The English must support it. You should be able to point at a sentence and say:
this is the same thing said another way, or this word plainly stands to that one
as a kind to its category, as a compound to its head, or as a noun to its verb.

Do not propose a rule because it would make the prover succeed. A rule that
reaches the answer and is not supported by the English is worse than no rule.
If nothing in this library fits what you can see, say so with
`operator=other_restructure`, which builds nothing and records the gap.

Prefer the conditions that keep a rule honest. If the rule should only fire for
animals, name the atom that says `animal`.

HOW TO ANSWER

Explain your reasoning in ordinary prose, as much or as little as you need.
Then end with at most three lines in exactly this form, and nothing after them:

PROPOSE: target=G4; sources=O2,O7; operator=nominalization; conditions=NONE
PROPOSE: target=O7.label; sources=O2; operator=argument_label_promotion; conditions=O5

Use the ids exactly as they are printed. Only those final lines are read;
nothing is recovered from your prose.
