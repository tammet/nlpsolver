Someone has proposed the rule below and provisionally accepted it. Your job is
to try to break it.

Assume it is wrong and look for the reason. Only if you genuinely cannot find
one should you let it pass. You are not told the problem's answer, and nothing
has been derived from this rule that you should try to protect; a rule that
would be convenient is not thereby correct.

HOW TO READ THE LOGIC

These are the notation's conventions.  They are facts about the notation, not
hints about this problem.

  isa(TYPE, THING)
      The FIRST argument names the type; the second is the individual or
      variable that is of that type.  `isa(bird, ?X)` says ?X is a bird.  It
      does NOT say bird is an ?X.

  is rel2(RELATION, X, Y)
      The first argument names the relation; X and Y are its two participants,
      in that order.  `is rel2(member of, ?X, C)` says ?X is a member of C.

  has property(PROPERTY, X)
      The first argument names the property; X is what has it.

  has degree property(PROPERTY, X, DEGREE, CLASS)
      As above, with a degree and the comparison class it belongs to.

  has type(E, WORD) / has actor(E, X) / has target(E, X)
      An event E, what kind it is, and who fills each role.

  forall ?X . forall ?Y . ( BODY -> normally HEAD )
      The rule fires for EVERY combination of ?X and ?Y that makes BODY true,
      and concludes HEAD for that combination.  It does NOT say that one thing
      stands in the relation to all of them.  `forall ?X . forall ?Y . (
      loves(?X, ?Y) & animal(?Y) -> normally animal_lover(?X) )` fires once for
      each pair where ?X loves an animal ?Y; it does not require ?X to love
      every animal.

  normally
      The conclusion holds by default and can be overridden by a contrary fact.
      It is not a claim of necessity.

  A name like "Tom 1" or a URL is one specific individual.  A `?X` is a
  variable: the rule speaks about everything that matches it.

Work through these six, in order, and say what you find under each:

1. DIRECTION AND SIGN
   Does the English license this direction, or only the converse? Does it
   license concluding the denial, or only the absence of a claim?

2. VARIABLES, CONSTANTS AND ARGUMENTS
   Is something quantified that the text only says of one named individual? Is
   something fixed to a name that the text says generally? Are the arguments in
   the positions the English puts them in — is it X about Y, or Y about X?

3. A MISSING CONDITION
   Does the English state the connection only under some circumstance that the
   rule does not require? Name it.

4. SCOPE
   The rule as written fires on everything matching its left side. Is that
   wider than what the text supports?

5. IS THIS AN ALIGNMENT AT ALL
   Are the two sides two ways of saying one thing, or two different things that
   often go together? A correlation, however reliable, is not a representation
   alignment.

6. A COUNTEREXAMPLE
   Describe a concrete situation consistent with everything the text says, in
   which the left side holds and the conclusion does not. If you can build one,
   the rule is broken. If you try and cannot, say what you tried.

Then end with exactly these two lines and nothing after them:

FINAL: PASS | FAIL | UNCERTAIN
FAULT: direction | sign | arguments | missing_guard | scope | not_an_alignment | counterexample | none

FAIL if you found a real fault. UNCERTAIN if you cannot decide. PASS only if you
attacked it under all six headings and it held. Only these two lines are read.
