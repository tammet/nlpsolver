An earlier selector marked a few pairs of expressions as worth examining. For
each one, decide whether a directional rule connecting them may be added.

Two things you should know about what happens next, because they change what
counts as a reasonable yes.

**The rule is defeasible and it is reported at low confidence.** It is added as
a default: it holds normally, a later fact can override it, and any answer that
leans on it is reported as depending on it. So you are not being asked whether
the two expressions are synonyms, or whether the rule is certain. You are asked
whether, read in this problem, it is a reasonable default in the direction
proposed.

**A translation repair is a separate question.** Sometimes two expressions do
not meet because one of them was written down badly, and the real fix is to
rewrite it. That is worth recording, and it is *not* a reason to refuse the
bridge: a local default bridge can be an admissible reading of the same
situation. Answer the two questions independently.

Reading the notation:

    isa(cat, ?X)                 ?X is a cat.  The class comes FIRST.
    is rel2(chases, ?X, ?Y)      ?X chases ?Y, in that order.
    has property(red, ?X)        ?X is red.
    has degree property(tall, ?X, none, person)
                                 ?X is tall; the last two arguments are the
                                 degree and the class compared against.
    has part(?X, ?Y)             ?Y is a part of ?X.
    ?X                           a universally quantified variable
    -isa(...)                    a negated atom
    normally                     holds by default; can be overridden

An atom marked *rule premise* is something a rule needs; *rule conclusion* is
something a rule produces; *question* is what is being asked.

Before you accept a rule that quantifies over everything, try to break it. Name
one concrete thing that would satisfy the left side and not the right. If you
find one, the answer is `conditional` and the restriction that excludes it goes
in CONDITIONS — or `no`, if no restriction you can name repairs it.

Three things that are not evidence:

  * **Similar words.** Sharing a stem says nothing about implication.
  * **Different words.** Neither does not sharing one.
  * **A plausible converse.** "Every surveyor inspects vessels" being believable
    is no reason to accept "everyone who inspects a vessel is a surveyor". Read
    the direction as printed and judge that one.

Some worked shapes, none of them from this problem:

  1. `isa(sloop, ?X) -> isa(vessel, ?X)`.  A sloop is a kind of vessel; nothing
     satisfies the left and fails the right.  BRIDGE: yes, SCOPE: general.
  2. `is rel2(handles, ?X, ?Y) -> isa("harbour worker", ?X)`.  Someone handling
     a crate is a counterexample.  BRIDGE: conditional, CONDITIONS: what is
     handled must be a vessel.
  3. `isa("harbour worker", ?X) -> is rel2(handles, ?X, ?Y)`.  Read literally
     this says a harbour worker handles every ?Y.  BRIDGE: no.
  4. Both sides are the same predicate with the same arguments: the theory
     already links them and no rule is needed.  BRIDGE: no, and say in WHY that
     the step is ordinary.
  5. `is rel2(moored, ?X, ?Y)` on one side and `is rel2("tied up", ?X, ?Y)` on
     the other, where one sentence was plainly written with the wrong relation
     name.  A repair is possible AND a local default bridge is admissible:
     BRIDGE: yes, SCOPE: local, REPAIR ALSO POSSIBLE: yes.
  6. A connection that is true but has nothing to do with what is being asked.
     Judge it on whether it is sound, not on whether it helps — you are not
     told what would help.

SCOPE means: `local`, the rule should mention only the individuals named in this
problem; `context`, it holds for this kind of situation; `general`, it holds of
the words themselves wherever they occur.

Use `uncertain` when you mean it. Nothing is built from an uncertain.
