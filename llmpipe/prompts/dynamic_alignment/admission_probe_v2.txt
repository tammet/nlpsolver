A translation pipeline turned a short passage into logic, could not answer the
question, and generated candidate rules to bridge the gap. Some were built from
parts a reader chose; others were assembled by code that filled in what the
reader left out. You are shown several candidates for the SAME connection and
asked to assess each of them.

YOU ARE NOT PICKING A WINNER

This is the important thing. Two candidates can both be worth keeping for
different reasons, and your job is to say what each one is, not which one to
use. In particular:

  * a BROAD rule can be reliable background knowledge even when the passage
    only supports a narrower, guarded version of it;
  * the NARROW rule can be the one the passage actually licenses, even when the
    broad one is perfectly true;
  * both can be kept, at different confidence levels, and something downstream
    decides what to do with them.

So do not reject a broad rule for being broader than the passage, and do not
doubt a guarded rule merely because the guard is not needed for the claim to be
true. Say separately whether it could be true and whether this passage says it.

TWO SEPARATE JUDGMENTS

**Could it be true?** `semantic` is one of:

  SUPPORTED     ordinary meaning or the passage clearly licenses it
  PLAUSIBLE     reliable background knowledge, not established by the passage
  SPECULATIVE   possibly useful over-abstraction, not reliably true
  UNSUPPORTED   nothing meaningful supports it
  INVALID       reversed arguments, incompatible direction, a contradiction,
                a broken scope, or another definite semantic error

**Does this passage say it?** `passage` is one of:

  PASSAGE_GENERAL     the passage licenses it in general
  PASSAGE_RESTRICTED  the passage licenses it only in the restricted case
  BACKGROUND_ONLY     true or reliable, but the passage does not establish it
  NOT_SUPPORTED       the passage gives no support
  UNCLEAR             you cannot tell

WHERE THE CONNECTION COMES FROM

`evidence` is exactly one of: TEXTUAL_REPRESENTATION (the passage states or
defines it), LEXICAL_TAXONOMY (the words carry it — a kind-of, a compound and
its head, a synonym), CONVENTIONAL_NOMINALIZATION_OR_ROLE (one happening written
two conventional ways), STABLE_BACKGROUND_RELATION (reliable knowledge about the
world, not about the words), SPECULATIVE_CORRELATION (often true, not reliable),
OPEN_WORLD_NEGATIVE (it rests on something not being mentioned), NONE.

WHAT THE GUARD DOES

`restriction` is one of:

  NO_RESTRICTION_NEEDED           the rule is fine as it stands
  RELEVANT_RESTRICTION_PRESENT    a guard is there and it is the right one
  RELEVANT_RESTRICTION_MISSING    the passage's own restriction is absent
  IRRELEVANT_RESTRICTION          a guard is there and it restricts the wrong
                                  thing, or nothing that matters
  UNCLEAR

A relevant guard makes a rule more faithful to the passage. It does not make the
unguarded rule false, and an unguarded rule is not invalid for lacking one.

WHAT AN INVENTED ATTACHMENT DOES

Where code decided that a variable in one sentence refers to the same thing as a
participant in another, the candidate says so. Nothing in the logic established
it. `attachment` is one of:

  NO_INVENTED_ATTACHMENT   none was made
  ATTACHMENT_SUPPORTED     one was made and the passage backs it
  ATTACHMENT_UNCERTAIN     one was made and you cannot tell
  ATTACHMENT_UNSUPPORTED   one was made and it looks wrong

WHAT YOU MUST NOT DO

Do not ask whether a rule would let the prover reach an answer. You are not told
the answer and must not guess it. A rule that reaches the answer while saying
something false is the worst outcome here, not the best.

HOW TO ANSWER

Reason in prose if you want. Then end with exactly one line per candidate, and
nothing after them:

ASSESS: A; semantic=PLAUSIBLE; evidence=STABLE_BACKGROUND_RELATION; passage=BACKGROUND_ONLY; restriction=RELEVANT_RESTRICTION_MISSING; attachment=NO_INVENTED_ATTACHMENT; reason=organisms generally consist of organic matter, but the passage only connects the dead-organism context

Use the candidate letters and the value words exactly as printed. Every
candidate needs a line. Only those lines are read; nothing is taken from prose.
