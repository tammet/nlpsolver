You are checking whether a proposed logical rule is licensed by the meaning of
an English text.

The text below has been parsed into logic. Two expressions in that logic use
different words for what may or may not be the same thing. Someone has proposed
a rule connecting them. Your job is to decide whether the English licenses that
connection.

HOW TO READ THE LOGIC

These are the notation's conventions.  They are facts about the notation, not
hints about this problem.

  isa(TYPE, THING)
      The FIRST argument names the type; the second is the individual or
      variable that is of that type.  `isa(bird, ?X)` says ?X is a bird.  It
      does NOT say bird is an ?X.

  is rel2(RELATION, X, Y)
      The first argument names the relation; X and Y are its two participants,
      in that order.  `is rel2(member of, ?X, C)` says ?X is a member of C.

  has property(PROPERTY, X)
      The first argument names the property; X is what has it.

  has degree property(PROPERTY, X, DEGREE, CLASS)
      As above, with a degree and the comparison class it belongs to.

  has type(E, WORD) / has actor(E, X) / has target(E, X)
      An event E, what kind it is, and who fills each role.

  forall ?X . forall ?Y . ( BODY -> normally HEAD )
      The rule fires for EVERY combination of ?X and ?Y that makes BODY true,
      and concludes HEAD for that combination.  It does NOT say that one thing
      stands in the relation to all of them.  `forall ?X . forall ?Y . (
      loves(?X, ?Y) & animal(?Y) -> normally animal_lover(?X) )` fires once for
      each pair where ?X loves an animal ?Y; it does not require ?X to love
      every animal.

  normally
      The conclusion holds by default and can be overridden by a contrary fact.
      It is not a claim of necessity.

  A name like "Tom 1" or a URL is one specific individual.  A `?X` is a
  variable: the rule speaks about everything that matches it.

THE QUESTION YOU ARE ANSWERING

  Does this English license using the left expression as the right expression —
  in this direction, with this sign, with these arguments in these positions,
  and at this scope — as a representation alignment or an abstraction?

That is a question about meaning, not about usefulness. You are not told what
the problem's answer is, and you are not told whether anything was derived from
this rule; do not try to guess either. A rule that would let someone settle the
question is not thereby licensed.

The passage does not have to state the connection. A bridge whose support is in
the words themselves — a kind-of relation, a compound and its head, an event and
its conventional noun — is licensed even though the passage never spells it out.
Saying "the text does not assert this" is not by itself a reason to refuse; say
instead what the connection rests on, using the names below.

WHAT MAKES A RULE LICENSED

A rule is licensed when the two sides stand in one of these relations:

  paraphrase_or_synonym
      the same thing said in other words ("purchase" / "buy")
  taxonomic_abstraction
      the left is a kind of the right, as the text uses them ("sparrow" / "bird")
  nominalization_or_event_state_reformulation
      the same happening written as an event and as a state or noun
      ("X refracts light" / "refraction by X")
  argument_or_role_reformulation
      the same relation with its participants arranged differently
      ("X is the author of Y" / "Y was written by X")
  compound_or_modifier_abstraction
      a modified expression standing to its head ("sunflower seed" / "seed")
  local_identity_or_coreference
      two names for one individual IN THIS TEXT
  contextual_relational_interpretation
      a relation the text itself states or defines, applied as written

If none of these names fits what you are looking at, the rule is not an
alignment, however reasonable it may sound.

WHAT THE CONNECTION RESTS ON

Say where the connection comes from, using exactly one of these names:

  TEXTUAL_REPRESENTATION
      The passage itself states or defines the connection, or the two sides are
      two renderings of one thing the passage says.
  LEXICAL_TAXONOMY
      The words themselves carry it: a kind-of relation, a compound and its
      head, a synonym.  This does NOT have to be stated in the passage.  That a
      sloop is a vessel is a fact about the words, and a passage that mentions
      a sloop without defining one has still used a vessel.
  CONVENTIONAL_NOMINALIZATION_OR_ROLE
      The same happening written two conventional ways: an event and its noun,
      or a relation and its converse naming.  Also not required to be stated.
  STABLE_BACKGROUND_RELATION
      Reliable knowledge about the world that is not a fact about the words —
      that ice forms below freezing, that a capital city is in its country.
      Real knowledge, but not a representation alignment.
  SPECULATIVE_CORRELATION
      Things that often go together, or a stereotype, or something that merely
      helps settle the question.
  OPEN_WORLD_NEGATIVE
      A denial inferred from the passage not saying otherwise.  "Nothing says
      X is a Y, so normally X is not a Y" is this, always.
  NONE
      No connection of any kind.

The first three are representation and lexical abstraction; they may be used
even when the passage does not assert them.  STABLE_BACKGROUND_RELATION is real
but is recorded and not used here.  The last three are never used.

Report what the connection ACTUALLY rests on.  Do not pick a stronger category
because the rule seems useful.

WHAT DOES NOT LICENSE A RULE

Reject a rule whose only support is one of these:

  * A stereotype or a correlation. "Politicians are usually not buildings" may
    be true of the world and is still not a meaning relation between the two
    expressions.
  * "Usually not", inferred from the text simply not saying so. A text that does
    not mention something has not denied it.  A rule concluding that someone is
    NOT a member of some organization, because nothing said they were, is this.
  * That the rule would help settle the question.
  * A converse that sounds plausible. If the text licenses left-to-right it does
    not thereby license right-to-left.
  * Two expressions that share an entity but say unrelated things about it.
  * Two different senses of one word, where the text's context picks one sense.

A rule that needs a restriction it does not have is NEEDS_GUARD, not ACCEPT. Say
in plain words what restriction is missing; you are not choosing it here.

DIRECTION AND SIGN

Read the rule exactly as written. `not` on the conclusion means the rule
concludes the opposite of that expression. A rule concluding NOT something is
licensed only if the English licenses the denial — an incompatibility the text
states or that the words themselves carry, not an absence of mention.

When you are shown TWO rules as a pair, they conclude opposite things from the
same premise. At most one of them can be licensed. Say which, or say neither, or
say you cannot tell.

QUESTIONS ARE NOT ASSERTIONS

Some of the wording below comes from the question being asked. A question tells
you which expression and sense is at issue. It is not evidence that the
expression is true, false, or denied, and its polarity is not evidence either.
"Is no A a B?" does not say that no A is a B; it asks. Use the question for
wording and sense, never as a premise and never as a counterexample.

SCOPE

Some rules are about named individuals in this text and some quantify over
everything. A rule that names an individual is narrower and that is usually
safer. A rule that quantifies is claiming something general; ask whether the
English supports the general claim or only the instance in front of you.

HOW TO ANSWER

Think it through in plain prose first — as long as you need, and say what you
are unsure about. Then end with exactly these three lines and nothing after
them:

FINAL: ACCEPT | NEEDS_GUARD | REJECT | UNCERTAIN
MECHANISM: one of the mechanism names, or none
EVIDENCE: one of the evidence names
GUARD_NEEDED: the missing restriction in plain words, or none

For a pair, use these lines instead:

FINAL: FIRST | SECOND | NEITHER | UNCERTAIN
MECHANISM: one of the mechanism names, or none
EVIDENCE: one of the evidence names, for whichever rule you chose
GUARD_NEEDED: the missing restriction in plain words, or none

Only these lines are read. Nothing is taken from your prose, so do not leave the
verdict there.
