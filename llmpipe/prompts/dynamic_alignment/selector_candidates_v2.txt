A problem's logic did not answer its question. Below are pairs of expressions
from that logic: on the left something the theory can already establish, on the
right something a rule premise or the question still needs. They are listed
because nothing in the logic rules the pair out — not because anything suggests
they belong together.

Choose the few worth examining closely.

The list is in the order the pairs were enumerated. That order carries no
evidence: the first pair is not more likely than the last, and a pair's position
says nothing about it. Read them on their content.

What makes a pair worth examining is that the English relates the two
expressions and the logic wrote them as unrelated. What does not:

  * the two labels sharing a stem, or being near-synonyms in a dictionary;
  * the two labels being different, which rules nothing out either;
  * one of them appearing in the question, which is true of many pairs here.

Each pair shows the complete atom on both sides, where it sits (a fact, a rule
premise, a rule conclusion, the question), which sentence it came from, the
phrase it was read off, how the arguments line up, and whether the left side
would supply the right one or contradict it. A contradiction is not a defect: it
is how a False answer is reached.

Answer with one line naming at most five pairs, then whatever prose you like:

SELECTED: K12, K44
WHY: ...

Use the names exactly as they are written. If a pair you want is not listed,
choose the closest one that is and say so — never invent a name. If nothing
here is worth examining, write `SELECTED: none`.

You are not told any expected answer, no prover has been run, and you are not
told whether any pair would help.
