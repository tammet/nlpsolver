A problem's logic has two expressions that do not meet: one the theory can
already establish, and one that something else in the problem needs. Someone has
proposed connecting them with a rule. Your job is to say what should be done
about that proposal.

You are not being asked whether the rule is certain. Any bridge that gets added
is added as a default: it holds normally and a later fact can override it. So do
not weigh strictness. Weigh two other things: what the rule needs in order not
to be wrong, and how far it should be allowed to reach.

Answer in exactly this form. Only the first line and the pair name are read
mechanically; everything else is prose.

ACTION: ADD_BRIDGE | ADD_CONDITIONAL_BRIDGE | REPAIR_TRANSLATION |
        ORDINARY | NO_DIRECT_BRIDGE | UNCERTAIN
PAIR: <the candidate name, or none>
DIRECTION: <in plain words, what follows from what>
CONDITIONS: <what must also hold, or none>
SCOPE: local | context | general | unclear
REASON: <a sentence or two>

What the actions mean:

ADD_BRIDGE
    the implication is sound as proposed and needs nothing added.

ADD_CONDITIONAL_BRIDGE
    the connection is real but the rule as proposed fires on things it should
    not. Name the missing restriction in CONDITIONS. If you cannot name it,
    this is not the action to choose.

REPAIR_TRANSLATION
    nothing about the world is missing; the problem is how one of the two
    expressions was written down. The fix is to change that representation so
    the two meet, not to invent background knowledge. Typical signs: a word
    recorded in the wrong kind of slot, one thing named twice, a relation
    written as the wrong relation.

ORDINARY
    no abstraction is needed. The two expressions are the same thing, or the
    theory already contains a rule that links them, and the step is routine.

NO_DIRECT_BRIDGE
    this implication is unsafe. **This does not mean the two concepts are
    unrelated.** It means that as a rule, in this direction, it would license
    conclusions the English does not support — and that no condition you can
    name repairs it.

UNCERTAIN
    the English does not settle it. Nothing is built from an UNCERTAIN, so use
    it when you mean it and not as a hedge.

SCOPE, when a bridge is proposed:

    local     the rule should mention the individuals in this problem and go no
              further — it is about these named things, not about the words in
              general.
    context   the rule holds for this kind of situation but not as a general
              fact about the words.
    general   the rule holds of the words themselves, wherever they occur.

Three things that are NOT evidence for a bridge:

  * **Similar words.** Two expressions sharing a stem, or being near-synonyms in
    a dictionary, says nothing about whether one implies the other here.
  * **The same label in two places.** A word used in one sentence and in another
    may be about different things, or may be a genuine repeat. Sameness of
    spelling is not sameness of reference, and if it IS a genuine repeat the
    step is ORDINARY, not a bridge.
  * **A plausible converse.** That "every surveyor inspects vessels" sounds true
    is no reason to accept "everyone who inspects a vessel is a surveyor", and
    accepting a rule because its opposite is believable is the commonest way to
    get the direction wrong. Read the proposed direction literally and judge
    that one.

You are not told any expected answer, no prover has been run, no proof is shown,
and it is not your job to say whether the connection would help answer the
question.
